It's night, and you're an archer on a castle wall. Down below, the enemy army is moving closer. Your captain walks along the wall with a pot of burning coals. "Fire arrows!" he shouts. You dip your arrow in, and the rag on the tip catches. You pull back the string, and the flame roars right next to your face. You let go. The arrow climbs into the dark sky like a tiny comet. And halfway there, it goes out. A thin line of smoke, and it drops, cold, into the grass.
Watch almost any medieval movie, and the sky is full of flaming arrows. Every siege. Every big battle. But in real history, fire arrows show up far less than you'd think. So why? Imagine setting your enemy on fire from a hundred meters away. Why wouldn't you do that every time?
Coming up, the Roman soldier who explains why you have to shoot them badly. The fire that gets worse when you throw water on it. The Viking funeral that never happened. And how the fire arrow didn't die. It changed its name and went to space.
Problem number one. The fire hates flying.
A war arrow leaves the bow faster than a car on a highway. Now imagine holding a candle out of a car window at that speed. That's your fire arrow. The wind blows it out.
The Romans knew this. Ammianus Marcellinus was a Roman soldier who wrote a history of his own time, about 1,600 years ago. He describes a Roman fire arrow. Its iron head is hollow, full of holes, and packed with burning fuel. And he gives one rule. You have to shoot it slowly, from a loose bow. Shoot it hard, and the fire goes out.
Think about what that means. Your enemy's normal arrows fly fast and far. Yours have to fly slow and short. So to hit him with fire, you have to walk closer, right into the range of his arrows. And all that fuel makes the front of your arrow heavy, so it doesn't fly straight either.
Problem number two. Lighting the thing.
You need a fire right next to you. In a line of thousands of archers, that means pots of burning coals every few steps. Next to wooden bows, bags of arrows, and men dressed in wool and linen. In the wind. Sounds safe, right?
And it's slow. A good archer can shoot around ten arrows a minute. Now add this. Dip the arrow. Wait for it to catch. Then pull back the string with a flame a few centimeters from your hand. Every fire arrow you shoot is two or three normal arrows you didn't. And if it rains? Forget it.
Problem number three, and this is the big one. What are you shooting at?
People don't catch fire like in the movies. A burning rag hits a soldier, and most of the time, it falls off or goes out. And the rag makes your point worse. A sharp steel point goes through cloth and skin. A ball of burning rag doesn't. So against people, a fire arrow is a worse arrow.
OK, so what about wooden walls? Think about lighting a campfire. You don't hold a match to a big log. You start with dry grass and small sticks. Thick wood needs a big, hot fire before it burns. A little flame on an arrow makes a black mark, and then it dies.
So fire arrows only work on one kind of target. Dry, thin things that burn easily. Straw roofs. Tents. Sails. Piles of hay. And that's exactly where history uses them. Not in open battles. In sieges.
Athens, four eighty four BC. A huge Persian army marches into the city. Most Athenians have already left. But a small group stays behind on the Acropolis, the rocky hill in the middle of the city. Why? Because of a prophecy. Before the war, the oracle at Delphi told the Athenians that a wooden wall would keep them safe. Most of them thought it meant ships. But these men take it literally. So they block the way up with wooden planks and doors, and they wait.
The Persians take a hill right across from them. Then, Herodotus says, they wrap their arrows in plant fiber and set them on fire. And they shoot them at the wooden wall. A wall of dry planks and old doors. The perfect target. It burns. The Persians climb up the hill, and the men on the Acropolis are killed. The wooden wall that was supposed to save them goes up in flames.
And the other wooden wall? The ships. Soon after, the Athenian fleet crushes the Persians at Salamis. So the oracle was right. Those men picked the wrong wall.
Now switch sides. You're defending the wall, and the enemy is shooting fire at it. What do you do?
Around three fifty BC, a Greek writer named Aeneas wrote a whole handbook on how to survive a siege. His advice? Cover the top of your walls with felt or raw animal skins, so they can't catch fire. Fresh hide doesn't burn easily. Attackers learned the same trick, and covered their siege towers and battering rams in fresh hides too.
There's even a stone carving from ancient Assyria, almost 3,000 years old. It shows defenders dropping torches onto a battering ram. And right below, soldiers pour water on the ram with long ladles.
But water isn't always the answer. Remember Ammianus? He says his Roman fire arrow had a nasty trick. Throw water on it, and the fire gets worse. The only way to put it out is to throw dirt on it.
So if fire on an arrow is so weak, how do you make fire scary? You make it bigger.
Saguntum, Spain, two hundred BC. Hannibal is attacking the city. The defenders have a weapon called the falarica. It's not an arrow. It's a heavy spear with an iron point about a meter long. They wrap it in fiber and pitch, set it on fire, and throw it. Even if it only hits a shield, the shield starts to burn. So the soldier has to throw his shield away, and now he's open to the next spear. The Roman historian Livy says it caused terror.
See the pattern? Fire works when it's big and heavy. On a little arrow, it's mostly a show.
Which brings us to a scene you've probably seen a hundred times. The Viking funeral. A dead king lies in his ship on the water. An archer shoots a burning arrow, and the ship bursts into flames as it drifts out to sea. Beautiful. And there's no proof it ever happened.
The best eyewitness we have is an Arab traveler named Ibn Fadlan. Around the year nine twenty two, he watched the funeral of a Rus chief. These were Scandinavian traders living on the Volga River. And the ship wasn't on the water. They pulled it up onto the land. And nobody shot an arrow. The dead man's closest relative walked up to the ship naked. Backwards. Holding a burning piece of wood. Then he set the ship on fire himself.
So, are flaming arrows a total failure? Not quite. Because in China, something happens that changes everything.
Around the year one thousand, Chinese soldiers start tying small tubes of a new powder to their arrows. Gunpowder. At first, it's the same old idea. Arrows that set things on fire. But over time, people notice something strange. When the powder burns inside a tube with one open end, it pushes. The arrow doesn't need a bow anymore. It flies by itself.
Remember the Roman problem? The faster it flies, the more the fire goes out. Now the fire is what makes it fly.
Korea takes this idea and builds a monster. The hwacha. A wooden cart that can fire around a hundred rocket arrows at once. In fifteen ninety three, at a mountain fortress called Haengju, a few thousand Korean defenders face about thirty thousand Japanese soldiers. With the help of their hwachas, the Koreans hold the fortress and win.
And here's my favorite part. What do the Chinese call these arrows? Huojian. Fire arrow. And what's the Chinese word for a rocket today? The same word. Huojian. When China sends astronauts into space, in Chinese, they're riding a fire arrow.
So why were flaming arrows almost never used? Because the fire hates flying. Because they're slow to light and dangerous to carry. Because people don't burn like straw, and thick walls don't burn like paper. Against a straw roof on a dry day, a fire arrow is a nightmare. Against an army in a field, it's a worse arrow. Movies love them because they look amazing on camera, especially at night. Real archers only wanted to hit you.
But the fire arrow got the last laugh. It stopped needing a bow, and now it flies to space.
