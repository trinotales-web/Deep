Sixty six million years ago, an asteroid about ten kilometers wide slams into the Earth. It wipes out the dinosaurs. And it lands right here, on the coast of what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. The land here is soft limestone, full of caves and holes. Around the edge of the buried crater, there's a ring of natural wells. They're full of fresh water, and they're called cenotes. In a land with almost no rivers, these wells mean life.
This is the Maya world. Southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, and parts of Honduras and El Salvador.
Around nine thousand years ago, people in Mexico start taming a wild grass. Over thousands of years, it turns into corn. And for the Maya, corn is everything. Their holy book, the Popol Vuh, says the gods tried three times to make people. First, they made them from mud. But the mud people fell apart. Next, they carved people from wood. But the wooden people had no hearts and no memory, and they forgot the gods. So the gods sent a flood, and the few who survived became monkeys. Finally, the gods made people from corn dough. And it worked. So the Maya are the people of corn. Remember that. We'll need it at the end.
By one thousand BC, the Maya are building big. In twenty twenty, scientists flying over Mexico with laser scanners found a giant hidden platform called Aguada Fénix. It's about one thousand four hundred meters long. That's longer than thirteen football fields. And it was built around three thousand years ago, with stone tools and human hands.
A few hundred years later, a city called El Mirador rises in the jungle of northern Guatemala. Up to two lakh people may live there. Its biggest pyramid, La Danta, is about seventy meters tall. It's shorter than the Great Pyramid of Egypt, but some say it holds even more stone.
Then, sometime around AD one fifty, El Mirador is abandoned. Why? One idea is that they cut down too many trees. To make the white plaster for their buildings, they burned huge amounts of wood. Without the forest, the good soil washed away. Keep that in mind. It won't be the last time a great Maya city empties.
After that, the Maya enter their golden age, from around AD two fifty to nine hundred. Cities like Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán and Caracol grow huge. In twenty eightteen, laser scans over Guatemala found more than sixty thousand hidden houses, walls and roads. Scientists now think ten to fifteen million people may have lived in the Maya lowlands.
But here's the strange part. There is no Maya empire. No emperor. No capital. Just dozens of kingdoms, each with its own holy king. They trade, they marry each other's daughters, and they fight all the time. So here's the rule. The Maya never have one king. Remember it, because it changes everything later.
Each king is called a holy lord. People believe he talks to the gods and keeps the rain coming. And the gods need blood. Royal blood. Kings pierce their own bodies with stingray spines. One famous carving shows a queen named Lady Xook. She's pulling a rope covered in thorns through her own tongue. Her husband, the king, holds a torch for her. Romantic.
And when they're not bleeding or fighting, the Maya are doing math. They have one of the most advanced writing systems of the ancient Americas. Some signs stand for whole words, others for sounds. They write on stone, on pots, and in folding books made of bark paper. They count in twenties, not tens, probably because they count fingers and toes. A dot means one. A bar means five. And a shell means zero. They're one of the first people on Earth to use zero.
And they're obsessed with time. They have a two hundred sixty-day calendar for rituals, and a three sixty five-day calendar for the seasons. Put them together, and they repeat every fifty two years. They also have a much longer count of days, called the Long Count. And that's where a famous myth comes from. One big cycle of the Long Count ends on December twenty, two thousand twelve. So people around the world panic. The Maya predicted the end of the world! Except they didn't. To the Maya, it's like a car's odometer rolling over to all zeros. A new cycle begins, and life goes on. And yes, we're still here.
And for fun? The Maya play ball. The ball is solid rubber. A ball can weigh up to three and a half kilos, heavy enough to break bones. You can't use your hands. You hit it with your hips. And on some courts, the goal is a stone ring high up on the wall.
The game has its own legend. In the Popol Vuh, two brothers play ball so loudly that they annoy the lords of the underworld. That's a dark place called Xibalba. The lords invite them down for a game, trick them, and kill them. They cut off one brother's head and hang it in a tree. Years later, his twin sons go down to the underworld to play the same lords. These are the Hero Twins. They survive every trap and beat the lords with clever tricks. Then they rise into the sky as the sun and the moon. Remember those twins.
So were real games life or death? You might have heard that the winners were sacrificed, as an honor. There's no good evidence for that. But prisoners of war were sometimes forced to play. And for them, losing could mean death.
Now, remember the rule? No empire. Only kingdoms. But by the five hundreds, two of them become superpowers. Tikal, in the jungles of Guatemala. And Calakmul, the Snake Kingdom, in what is now Mexico. Calakmul is clever. It makes friends with Tikal's neighbors. And in five sixty two, one of those friends, a city called Caracol, attacks Tikal with Calakmul's help. Experts call it a "star war," after the sign the Maya carved for it.
Tikal loses. Its king is probably captured and sacrificed. And for about one thirty years, Tikal goes quiet. No new monuments. No big buildings.
But Tikal doesn't forget. In six ninety, a new Tikal king named Jasaw Chan K'awiil marches against Calakmul. He beats Calakmul's king, a man called Fire Claw, and carries home Calakmul's giant war god statue. Today, Jasaw lies buried under Tikal's most famous pyramid, the Temple of the Great Jaguar.
Meanwhile, the city of Palenque has its own star. K'inich Janaab Pakal. He becomes king at twelve, and he rules for sixty eight years. When he dies at eighty, he's buried deep inside a pyramid. Then the Maya fill the stairway to his tomb with rubble, so nobody can get in. It works for almost one thousand three hundred years.
In nineteen forty nine, a Mexican archaeologist named Alberto Ruz finds a hidden stairway under the temple floor. Then, in nineteen fifty two, they lift a huge stone, and there he is. Pakal, covered in jade, wearing a mask made of jade pieces. His stone coffin lid shows Pakal leaning back, surrounded by strange shapes. In the nineteen sixty's, a writer claimed it shows an astronaut flying a rocket. But today, experts can read Maya symbols. It shows Pakal at the moment of death, sinking into the underworld. Above him rises the tree of life. Not a rocket. A tree.
And then, in the eight hundred's, it all starts to fall apart. In the south, one great city after another stops building. The last known Long Count date is carved in the year nine oh nine. Then, silence. Cities like Tikal and Palenque slowly empty, and the jungle swallows them.
So what happened? Scientists studying the mud at the bottom of a lake in Mexico found part of the answer. Drought. Between about seven fifty and one thousand fifty, rainfall drops by about half. At its worst, by up to seventy percent. The holy king's job is to keep the rain coming. And the rain stops. Wars get worse. There are too many people, too few trees, and not enough rain. People lose faith in their kings, and they leave.
But here's what most people get wrong. The Maya don't vanish. Remember the rule? There's no empire to collapse. When the southern cities die, the people walk away and start again. Many head north, to the Yucatán.
And there, a new city becomes the star. Chichén Itzá. Its name means "at the mouth of the well of the Itza." Yes, the same Itza from our island. Their own stories say their ancestors came from here. Its main pyramid, El Castillo, is a giant calendar. Each of its four sides has ninety one steps. Add the step at the top, and you get three sixty five. One for every day of the year. And at the spring and autumn equinoxes, the afternoon sun casts a shadow on the stairs. It looks like a giant snake sliding down the pyramid.
The city is built next to a huge cenote, and people threw offerings into it. Gold, jade, and sometimes humans. For a long time, people believed the Maya mostly sacrificed young women here. But in twenty twenty four, scientists tested the DNA of sixty four children. They were buried in an underground chamber near the sacred cenote. Every one of them was a boy. Most were between three and six years old. And among them were two pairs of identical twins. Remember the Hero Twins? Some researchers think these boys were sacrificed in rituals linked to the twins' story.
Later, a city called Mayapán rules the north. But in the fourteen hundred's, its own leading families tear it apart. The north breaks into more than a dozen small kingdoms.
In fifteen oh two on his last voyage, Christopher Columbus meets a big Maya trading canoe off Honduras. On board are goods to sell, including cacao beans. Columbus thinks they're almonds. He has no idea he's looking at chocolate.
Then, in fifteen eleven, a Spanish ship sinks off the coast of the Yucatán. A few survivors wash ashore, and the Maya capture them. Most of them die. But two survive for years. A priest named Gerónimo de Aguilar, and a sailor named Gonzalo Guerrero.
Guerrero does something nobody expects. He becomes Maya. He marries the daughter of a Maya lord and has three children. He tattoos his face, pierces his ears, and becomes a war captain.
In fifteen nineteen, Hernán Cortés lands nearby and sends for the two Spaniards. Aguilar comes running. He becomes Cortés's translator, and helps him conquer the Aztecs. But Guerrero says no. "Brother Aguilar," he says, "I am married and have three children. My face is tattooed and my ears are pierced." He stays, and he fights the Spanish for years. In fifteen thirty six he dies in battle in Honduras, on the Maya side.
Then the Spanish come for real. And here, the rule changes everything. The Aztecs have one capital, Tenochtitlan. Cortés takes it in fifteen twenty one, and the whole empire falls in about two years. But the Maya have no capital to take. Dozens of kingdoms. Each one has to be beaten, one at a time.
Diseases like smallpox often arrive before the soldiers, and they kill huge numbers of people. In fifteen twenty four, a Spanish captain named Pedro de Alvarado invades the highlands of Guatemala. Legend says a K'iche' prince named Tecun Uman fights him face to face. When he falls, a quetzal bird lands on his chest. That, the story goes, is why the quetzal's chest is red. In the north, it takes the Spanish about twenty years just to take the Yucatán.
And in fifteen twenty five, on a long march through the jungle, Cortés passes the island city of the Itza. He leaves behind a lame horse, and promises to come back for it. The Itza have never seen a horse. So they treat it like a god. They feed it birds, meat and flowers. The horse dies. Afraid of being blamed for killing a god, the Itza make a statue of it and worship it. They call it Tzimin Chac. The thunder horse. Almost a hundred years later, in sixteen eighteen, two Spanish friars visit and find the statue. One of them smashes it to pieces. Not a great way to make friends.
Meanwhile, in the north, a Spanish priest named Diego de Landa wants to wipe out the old religion. In July fifteen sixty two, in the town of Maní, he burns thousands of Maya idols and twenty seven Maya books. Landa later wrote, "We burned them all, which they regretted to an amazing degree." Centuries of Maya knowledge, gone in smoke. Today, only four Maya books survive in the whole world.
But here's the twist. Years later, Landa writes a book about the Maya. In it, with help from a Maya noble, he tries to write down a Maya "alphabet." It's mostly wrong. But in the nineteen fifty's, a Russian scholar named Yuri Knorozov looks again. He realizes that Landa's alphabet is a list of sounds. That's the key. It helps unlock Maya writing. So the man who burned the Maya books also helped us read them.
Now, back to that island. By the sixteen ninety's, the Itza kingdom is the last free Maya kingdom. Hidden in the jungle behind a lake, it has kept the Spanish out for over one seventy years. Then the Spanish bring a big warship to the edge of the lake. On March thirteen, sixteen ninety seven, it sails toward the island with its cannons. The Itza paddle out in more than three hundred canoes. But arrows can't stop cannons. Within a few hours, the city falls. The Spanish burn the temples, and soon they capture the king, Kan Ek'.

But that's not the end of the Maya. Not even close. In eighteen forty seven, the Maya of the Yucatán rise up against their rulers. It's called the Caste War.
