Imagine you're an engineer at ISRO's mission control in Bengaluru, India. It's August 23, 2023. On your screen, a small lander called Vikram is falling toward the Moon. Four days ago, Russia's lander, Luna-25, tried to reach this same part of the Moon. It lost control and smashed into the surface. Now it's India's turn. The room goes quiet. The lander slows down. It turns upright. And at 6:04 in the evening, it touches down. India becomes the first country ever to land near the Moon's south pole.
So why are so many countries racing to the same spot? The darkest, coldest, most dangerous part of the Moon? Coming up, a giant mystery buried under the Moon. Craters that haven't seen sunlight in billions of years. A rocket smashed into the Moon on purpose. Mountains where the Sun almost never sets. And a "trillion-dollar fuel" that might not be the real prize at all. But first, we go back about 4 billion years, to the day something huge hit the Moon.
A giant space rock, maybe around 160 kilometers wide, slams into the Moon's far side. It leaves behind one of the biggest craters in the whole solar system. Today, we call it the South Pole-Aitken basin. It's about 2,500 kilometers wide. That's big enough to cover all of India. And in places, it's about 8 kilometers deep. Almost as deep as Mount Everest is tall.
For a long time, that was all we knew about it. Then, in 2011, NASA sends two small spacecraft to the Moon, named Ebb and Flow. They fly one behind the other. As they pass over heavier parts of the Moon, the front one gets pulled a tiny bit faster. By measuring the gap between them, scientists can map the Moon's gravity. It's like an X-ray of the whole Moon. And when the mission ends, the two spacecraft are crashed into a mountain on purpose.
In 2019, scientists at Baylor University in Texas study that gravity map. And under the South Pole-Aitken basin, they find something strange. A huge, heavy mass, hidden deep below the surface. It's so heavy that it pulls the crater floor down by almost a kilometer. One scientist described it like this. Imagine a pile of metal five times bigger than the Big Island of Hawaii, buried underground.
So what is it? Maybe it's the iron and nickel core of the space rock that made the crater. And it's still stuck inside the Moon. Or maybe it's heavy material from long ago, when the young Moon was covered in an ocean of melted rock. Nobody knows for sure. Remember this mystery. We'll come back to it.
But the metal isn't why countries are racing here. The real reason is hiding in the shadows.
Earth is tilted, which is why we have seasons. But the Moon is hardly tilted at all. Just about one and a half degrees. So at the Moon's poles, the Sun never climbs high in the sky. It just slides along the horizon. That means the bottoms of some deep craters never get any sunlight at all. Not for a day. Not for a year. Some of them haven't seen the Sun in billions of years.
And without sunlight, they get cold. Unbelievably cold. Some of these craters drop below minus 230 degrees Celsius. That's colder than the surface of Pluto. Scientists call them cold traps. Because anything that falls in, stays in.
And over billions of years, a lot has fallen in. Comets and asteroids crash into the Moon, bringing water with them. The Sun also blasts the Moon with tiny particles. Some of them help make water in the soil. A 2023 study even suggests that Earth itself may be helping, with particles from our planet's magnetic field. Most of this water quickly escapes into space. But some of it bounces around the Moon until it lands in a cold trap. And there, it freezes. Maybe forever.
Here's the funny part. When the Apollo astronauts brought Moon rocks back to Earth, scientists decided the Moon was bone dry. For decades, that's what everyone believed.
Then, in 2008, India launches its first mission to the Moon, Chandrayaan-1. On board is a NASA instrument that studies the Moon's surface. And in 2009, it finds signs of water. Not lakes or rivers. Tiny amounts, locked in the soil, across much of the Moon.
That same year, NASA tries something bold. It takes a used rocket stage, weighing more than two tonnes. Then it crashes it into a dark crater near the south pole, called Cabeus. It hits as fast as a speeding bullet. A second spacecraft flies through the cloud of dust it throws up, and studies it. The result? The cloud has water in it. Later studies say about 5 percent of the material was water.
Another NASA radar on Chandrayaan-1 finds ice in more than 40 small craters near the north pole. At least 600 million tonnes of it. And in 2018, scientists look at Chandrayaan-1's data again. They find the first direct proof of ice on the surface, inside the shadowed craters. So some of the biggest clues about the Moon's water came from India's first Moon mission.
And this brings us to the rule of the new space race. In space, water is worth more than gold. Remember it, because it explains everything that comes next.
Why is water so valuable? Because sending anything from Earth to the Moon is expensive. Every bottle of water you carry costs a fortune. But if you can find water on the Moon, you can drink it. You can split it into hydrogen and oxygen. The oxygen lets you breathe. And hydrogen and oxygen together make rocket fuel. The Moon's gravity is only about one sixth of Earth's. So it's much easier to launch a rocket from there. The Moon could become a gas station for space.
But a base also needs power. And here, the south pole has another trick. Remember how the Sun slides along the horizon? Some high crater rims and peaks near the south pole get sunlight most of the time. People call them the "peaks of eternal light." That's not quite true. Even the best spots get dark sometimes. But they're sunny most of the time, which makes them perfect for solar panels. So right next to the darkest, iciest craters, you have some of the sunniest spots on the Moon. Water and power, side by side.
There's just one problem. Landing there is hard. The ground is rough, full of boulders and steep slopes. And the long shadows make it hard for cameras to see. Russia's Luna-25 crashed. In 2024, an American company's lander, Odysseus, touches down near the south pole. Then it tips over onto its side. In 2025, another one, Athena, lands in a crater and falls over too. So when Vikram landed upright in 2023, it was a big deal.
Now, the big players are getting ready. In April 2026, NASA's Artemis II flies four astronauts around the Moon. They travel farther from Earth than any humans ever have. But nobody lands. NASA now plans to test its landers in Earth orbit in 2027. Then, in 2028, it wants to put astronauts back on the Moon. And where does it want to go? The south pole.
China is moving fast too. In 2019, it lands the first spacecraft ever on the Moon's far side, inside the South Pole-Aitken basin. In 2024, its Chang'e-6 mission brings back the first rocks from the far side. Its next mission, Chang'e-7, is going to the edge of Shackleton, a crater near the south pole. Its job is to hunt for ice. It even carries a small robot that can hop into dark craters. It was supposed to launch in August 2026. But a typhoon and a tricky landing schedule pushed it back to 2027. China says it wants to land its own astronauts before 2030. And with Russia, it plans to build a research base near the south pole.
Remember the rule? Water is worth more than gold. And there aren't many places where ice and sunlight sit this close together. That's why everyone wants the same small area.
So who owns the Moon? Legally, nobody. A treaty signed in 1967 says no country can claim the Moon or any part of it. But a country can build a base, and ask others to keep a safe distance. And if the best spot is already taken, you can't exactly ask them to move. So some experts worry this could turn into a race of "first come, first served."
Now, you might have heard that the real prize on the Moon is helium-3. A rare gas that could power fusion reactors, worth trillions of dollars. Let's check that. Helium-3 is real, and the Moon has more of it than Earth. But it's spread thin in the dusty soil. You'd have to dig up and heat huge amounts of moon dirt to get a little. And the fusion power plants that could burn it? They don't exist yet. Plus, helium-3 isn't mainly a south pole thing. It builds up in soil that gets hit by sunlight. The south pole's prize is still water.
But there's a twist. Helium-3 might matter for a different reason. Quantum computers need it to get extremely cold. In 2025, the US government agreed to buy three liters of helium-3 from a company called Interlune. It's supposed to be harvested from the Moon and delivered by 2029. The company says it sells for about 20 million dollars a kilo. So helium-3 may not power the world. But it might help power the next generation of computers.
So why is everyone racing to the Moon's south pole? Remember the rule? In space, water is worth more than gold. And the south pole has ice that built up over billions of years. It sits in craters where the Sun never shines, right next to peaks where it almost never sets.
Remember Vikram, landing upright in 2023? It was the first to land near the south pole. It won't be the last. And deep under that giant crater, the mystery mass is still waiting. Metal from an ancient space rock? Or something else? We still don't know.
