Imagine you're a French soldier on a mountain road near Grenoble. It's March 7, 1815. Your musket is loaded, and your orders are clear. Stop Napoleon.
Just over a week ago, he escaped from the island where he was being kept. Now he's marching on Paris to take back his throne. And he's walking straight toward you. Alone.
He stops in front of your line and opens his coat. "Soldiers," he calls out. "If there is one among you who wants to kill his emperor, here I am." An officer shouts, "Fire!" Your finger is on the trigger.
So how does a boy from a small island become the most powerful man in Europe? A boy who barely spoke French? And how does he lose it all? Not once, but twice.
Coming up: a schoolboy mocked for his accent, a general who almost became a romance writer, a famous painting with a secret mule, a short emperor who wasn't short, and the biggest land sale in history. But first, we go back to where it all began. Corsica, 1769.
Corsica is a rocky island in the Mediterranean. In 1768, the city of Genoa sells it to France. The Corsicans fight back. Among the rebels is a young couple, Carlo and Letizia Buonaparte. Letizia is pregnant. But the French win, and on August 15, 1769, she gives birth to a boy. Napoleon.
His father makes a smart move. He switches sides and makes friends with the French. That gets the family a place among the lower nobility. So when Napoleon is nine, he's sent to school in France. There's one problem. He barely speaks French. He grew up speaking Corsican and Italian. The other boys laugh at his accent and his strange name. He has few friends. So he reads: history, math, and the lives of great generals. He keeps his Corsican accent for the rest of his life.
At 16, he becomes an artillery officer. That same year, his father dies of stomach cancer. Remember that. It matters at the end.
Then, in 1789, everything changes. The French Revolution. The king loses his throne, and later his head. Many noble officers flee the country. Suddenly, a talented young officer from Corsica has a chance.
In 1793, rebels hand the port city of Toulon to the British navy. Napoleon, just 24, sees what older officers missed. Take a small fort on a hill, and your cannons can hit the British ships in the harbor. He leads the attack himself and takes a bayonet in the thigh. But it works. The British sail away, and Napoleon is made a general at 24.
Then, his career stalls. He's given a job he doesn't want, and he refuses it. For a while, he has no command at all. So what does he do? He writes a romance story. It's called Clisson and Eugénie. The hero is a brilliant, lonely young soldier. Sound familiar?
Then, in October 1795, thousands of armed royalists march on Paris to bring back the king. The government panics, and someone remembers the young general from Toulon. Napoleon brings in cannons and points them down the streets around the government's palace. When the royalists attack, the cannons fire. The attack collapses within hours. The romance writer has just saved the Revolution.
As a reward, he gets his own army, in Italy. And here we see Napoleon's rule for the first time. He wins by being faster than everyone else. Faster to think, faster to march, faster to strike. Remember this rule. It explains his greatest wins and his worst disasters.
His army in Italy is hungry, unpaid, and dressed in rags. Within weeks, he has it moving faster than the enemy can react. He splits his enemies apart and beats them one by one. His soldiers joke that he wins wars with their legs. And they give him a nickname: the little corporal.
And no, that's not because he was short. Let's bust that myth right now. Napoleon was about 1.68 meters tall. That's average, or a bit above, for a Frenchman back then. The myth comes partly from old French inches, which were longer than English ones. It comes from the giant guards who stood next to him. And it comes from British cartoonists, who drew him as a tiny, angry man called Little Boney.
In about a year, he wins battle after battle, and Austria is forced to make peace.
Then, in 1798, he sails to Egypt with tens of thousands of soldiers. The plan? Hurt Britain by threatening its route to India. He also brings more than 150 scientists. Near the pyramids, he crushes the army that rules Egypt. But days later, the British admiral Horatio Nelson finds the French fleet and destroys it. Remember the rule? Without ships, Napoleon can't move. He's trapped. His army fights on through the desert and a plague. In 1799, he sneaks back to France on a small ship, leaving most of his army behind.
His scientists do find something amazing, though. French soldiers dig up a stone with the same message written in three scripts. The Rosetta Stone. It becomes the key to reading Egyptian hieroglyphs. And where is it today? In London. The British took it as a prize of war.
Back home, France is losing a new war, and the government is weak. In November 1799, Napoleon takes power in a coup. He's 30 years old, and he now rules France.
The next spring, he does it again. He leads an army over the Alps to surprise the Austrians in Italy. You've probably seen the painting. Napoleon on a rearing horse, cape flying in the wind. In real life, he crossed on a mule. But he wanted to be painted calm on a fiery horse, so that's what the painter gave him.
Now, ruling France, he changes almost everything. He creates a new set of laws, the Napoleonic Code. Under it, men are equal before the law, no matter what family they're born into. Versions of it still shape the laws of many countries today.
But there's a dark side too. His laws treat wives as if they must obey their husbands. And in 1802, he brings back slavery in France's colonies. In Haiti, the people fight back and beat his army. In 1804, Haiti becomes independent.
Losing Haiti also wrecks his plans for America. So in 1803, he sells a huge piece of land to the United States. The Louisiana Purchase. For $15 million, the US doubles in size. It's the biggest land deal in history.
In 1804, at age 35, he becomes emperor. At the ceremony in Paris, the pope is there. But when it's time for the crown, Napoleon picks it up and puts it on his own head.
Exactly one year later, on December 2, 1805, he wins his greatest battle. Austerlitz. He's outnumbered by Russian and Austrian armies. So he pretends to be weak. He lets the enemy attack his weaker side. Then his troops charge out of the morning fog and cut the enemy army in two. Remember the rule? Some of his men marched about 110 kilometers in two days just to be there in time.
But slowly, the rule turns against him. In 1808, he invades Spain and gets stuck fighting guerrillas who hit and run. You can't win fast against an enemy who won't stand and fight.
And then comes his biggest mistake. Russia. In June 1812, he invades with around 600,000 men. The Russians don't stand and fight. They pull back and burn the land behind them. Napoleon chases them deeper and deeper. In September, he reaches Moscow. Soon, the city is on fire. He waits five weeks for the Tsar to surrender. The Tsar never does. So in October, Napoleon turns back. Then the snow comes.
But here's a myth. The winter didn't destroy his army on its own. Disease killed huge numbers too. In 2025, scientists studied DNA from soldiers buried in a mass grave in Lithuania. They found fevers spread by dirty water and by lice. Of the roughly 600,000 men who marched into Russia, only a small fraction came home.
After that, all of Europe turns on him. In 1814, his enemies take Paris, and he's forced to give up his throne. They send him to Elba, a small island off Italy. He even gets to keep the title of emperor. Of an island.
Ten months later, he escapes. He lands in France with about 1,000 men and heads for Paris. And near Grenoble, he meets that regiment. Remember the soldier with his finger on the trigger? The order to fire comes. Nobody fires. Instead, the soldiers shout, "Long live the Emperor!" and join him. Within weeks, he's back in Paris, and the king has fled.
But it doesn't last. On June 18, 1815, near a Belgian village called Waterloo, he faces a British army under the Duke of Wellington. And a Prussian army is on its way to help them. It rained all night, and the ground is mud. Napoleon waits for it to dry before he attacks. Hours pass. Remember the rule? This time, he's slow. And those hours let the Prussians arrive. By evening, his army is broken. Wellington later calls it "the nearest run thing you ever saw in your life."
This time, the British take no chances. They send him to Saint Helena, a tiny island in the middle of the South Atlantic. He dies there on May 5, 1821. He's 51.
For years, people said he was poisoned with arsenic. In 2008, scientists in Italy tested his hair from childhood, from exile, and from the day he died. The arsenic levels were about 100 times higher than in people today. But they were high his whole life. Back then, arsenic was all around. Most experts think he died of stomach cancer. Remember his father? The same disease.
So how did a lonely boy from Corsica conquer most of Europe? Remember the rule? He was faster than everyone else. Faster to see a chance, faster to march, faster to strike. One historian counts about 60 battles in his career. He lost only seven. But when the sea, the mud, or the endless Russian land slowed him down, he lost everything.
