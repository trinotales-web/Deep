It's spring 1945. You're 19 years old, standing on an airfield in the south of Japan. In a few hours, you'll fly a plane loaded with a bomb into an American warship. There's no plan to come back. Before you climb in, a young doctor rolls up your sleeve and gives you a shot. "This will wake you up," he says. Neither of you knows what's inside. It's meth.
So were Japan's kamikaze pilots drugged to make them brave enough to die? Or is the real story stranger? Coming up, a chocolate bar stamped with the emperor's symbol. A doctor who didn't know what he was injecting. And a drug that outlived the war. But first, the drug itself. Because it starts in Japan.
In 1893, a Japanese chemist studying a plant used in Chinese medicine makes a new chemical. Nobody pays much attention. Then, in 1941, a Japanese company starts selling it in pharmacies. They call it Hiropon. The name comes from a Greek word that means "love of work." The ads promise it will chase away tiredness and sleep. So in wartime Japan, meth isn't a street drug. It's a product, like a much stronger cup of coffee.
And the army and navy love it. Pilots take it on long flights. Guards take it on night watch. Factory workers take it to get through night shifts.
Now jump to the end of 1944. Japan is losing the war. So its commanders turn to a desperate idea. Fill planes with bombs, and crash them into enemy ships. The world calls these pilots kamikaze, the "wind of the gods." In Japan, they're called tokko, special attack units. Most of the pilots are teenagers or in their early twenties. Many have barely learned to fly. And the flight to Okinawa, where the American fleet is waiting, takes two hours or more. For the generals, a sleepy pilot means a wasted plane.
So how do they keep them sharp? The first clue comes from a schoolgirl, only about 14.
Her name is Umeda Kazuko. In February 1945, she moves to a girls' school in Ibaraki, near Osaka. But there are almost no lessons. The school has been turned into a food factory for the army. Her job is to wrap chocolate. Each piece is a thick stick about 15 centimeters long. And each one is stamped with the emperor's symbol, a chrysanthemum flower. One day, the older girls tell her to try a piece. "The moment I ate it," she said later, "I felt a hot rush." When she tells her father, he says, "There's probably Hiropon in it." The teachers say the chocolate is a gift for soldiers. The older girls say something darker. It's for the special attack pilots, to eat before they die.
And here's the strange part. No written record of this chocolate has been found. One researcher thinks that's no accident. After the war, nobody wanted to talk about it.
The second clue comes from the man holding the needle.
His name is Kambara Hiroshi. In 1945, he's a 21-year-old navy doctor at Kushira, an air base in Kagoshima. When the battle of Okinawa begins, his officers give him an order. Before each mission, inject the pilots. The medicine comes in small brown glass tubes. "It will wake them up," he's told. So he does it. Again and again, until the war ends. By his own count, at least 200 young men.
He remembers how quiet they are. As if they've already said goodbye to life. One pilot looks at him and says, "You're lucky, doctor. You don't have to die." He has no answer. Only after the war does he understand what was in those brown tubes. Hiropon.
So, were the kamikaze drugged into dying? Not the way the myth says. Meth doesn't make you want to die. It doesn't control your mind. It keeps you awake, alert and pumped up. That's it. These pilots were pushed long before anyone touched their arm. By their officers. By their training. By a whole country telling them that dying for the emperor was the greatest honor. The shot only made sure they didn't fall asleep on the way.
And Japan wasn't the only one. German soldiers marched on pills called Pervitin. British and American bomber crews took Benzedrine. World War Two, it turns out, ran on speed.
But Japan's story has one more twist. When the war ends in August 1945, the military has warehouses full of Hiropon. Instead of being destroyed, much of it ends up on the black market. Soldiers, workers, people trying to survive in burned-out cities. They all want it. By one estimate, around two million Japanese try it in the ten years after the war. About 200,000 get hooked. In 1951, Japan finally bans it. The drug made to fight one war came home and started another.
And Kambara? He regretted those shots for the rest of his life. "I did a terrible thing," he said. In March 2025, he died at the age of 101. He left behind a short poem. It roughly means, "Life is as short as morning dew. I leave behind one wish. No more war."
