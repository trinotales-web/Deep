Imagine you're a scientist in a small boat off the coast of California. It's 2018. Next to you, a dark back rises out of the water. And it keeps rising. It's almost as long as a basketball court. Your teammate leans out with a long pole. With suction cups, they stick a small sensor onto the animal's back. It's going to do something nobody has ever done before. Record the heartbeat of a blue whale. The whale breathes, then dives. Hours later, you look at the data. Deep underwater, its giant heart is beating just twice a minute.
This is the biggest animal on Earth. It can grow to about 33 meters long and weigh around 150 tonnes. Its heart alone weighs about 180 kilos. Even a newborn baby weighs about 2.5 tonnes. As far as we know, it's the heaviest animal that has ever lived. Heavier than any dinosaur.
So how did an animal get this big? And why this one? Coming up, a whale that walks on four legs. A fossil that was supposed to beat the blue whale, until someone checked the math. A mouthful worth half a million calories. And the hunters who almost wiped the giants out. But first, we go back 50 million years, to a riverbank in what is now Pakistan.
Meet Pakicetus. It's about the size of a wolf. It has four legs, a long tail, and sharp teeth. It hunts on land, and it hangs around near water. It doesn't look anything like a whale. But it is one. How do we know? Its ear bones have a special shape that only whales have. Pakicetus is one of the earliest whales we know of. The great-grandfather of the giants.
And here's a fun fact. The closest living cousin of whales today? The hippo.
Over millions of years, Pakicetus's family moves deeper into the water. Their legs shrink. Their bodies get longer. Their nostrils move to the top of their heads and become blowholes. By about 40 million years ago, some whales live only in the sea.
And some of them are big. In 2023, scientists announced a 39-million-year-old fossil whale from Peru, called Perucetus. They said it might have been even heavier than a blue whale. Headlines everywhere said the blue whale had lost its crown. Then, a year later, two other scientists checked the math. Their answer? Perucetus probably weighed around 60 to 70 tonnes. Big, but nowhere near a blue whale. The crown stays where it is.
Around 34 million years ago, the whale family splits in two. On one side are the toothed whales, like dolphins and sperm whales. They hunt one animal at a time. On the other side are the baleen whales. Instead of teeth, they grow plates of baleen. It's made of keratin, the same stuff as your fingernails. Baleen works like a giant sieve. The whale takes in water full of food, pushes the water out, and keeps the food.
Now here's the strange part. Baleen whales have been around for more than 30 million years. But for most of that time, they aren't giants. Most of them are much smaller than the whales we see today. So when did they become huge?
In 2017, a team of scientists measured fossil whale skulls in a museum in Washington. The width of a whale's skull tells you roughly how long the whole whale was. And what they found surprised them. Whales longer than 10 meters only start to appear about 4.5 million years ago. The true giants arrive even later, around 2 to 3 million years ago. In whale history, that's yesterday. As one of the scientists put it, "We live in a time of giants."
So what changed? The Ice Ages. As giant ice sheets grow in the north, the oceans change. Before, food was spread out fairly evenly across the sea. Now, melting ice and strong winds bring nutrients up to the surface. It happens in certain places, at certain times of year. Tiny plants bloom. And the animals that eat them gather in enormous, crowded swarms.
And this brings us to the rule that made the giants. For a whale that eats by filtering, size pays. The bigger you are, the more food you get from every mouthful. Remember this rule. It explains almost everything about the blue whale.
The blue whale belongs to a group called rorquals. They feed in a way called lunge feeding. The whale speeds toward a cloud of food and opens its mouth wide. Its lower jaw swings open, and its throat has long folds that stretch like a balloon. In one gulp, it can take in a volume of water about as big as its own body. Then it closes its mouth, pushes the water out through its baleen, and swallows the food.
Each lunge costs a lot of energy. But scientists worked out that a single blue whale mouthful can hold up to about 443,000 calories. That's about 240 times more energy than the whale spends to take it. Or, if you like, the same energy as about 800 burgers. In one bite.
And what's in that bite? Krill. Blue whales eat almost nothing else. Krill are small, shrimp-like animals, about the size of your little finger. So here's the funny part. The biggest animal that has ever lived survives by eating one of the smallest.
But krill come in huge swarms, packed tightly together. And remember the rule? The bigger the mouth, the more krill in every gulp. A 2021 study found that a blue whale can eat up to 16 tonnes of krill. In a single day.
There's a catch, though. Krill don't stay around all year. In summer, they explode in cold waters near the poles. Then they become hard to find. So blue whales eat as much as they can for a few months. Then they swim thousands of kilometers to warmer waters to give birth. On that trip, they don't eat much. They live on the fat they stored up.
And here, being big helps again. A bigger body is a bigger fuel tank. The more fat you can carry, the longer you can go without food. So the rule gets even stronger. Bigger mouth, more food. Bigger body, more fuel.
That fat also feeds the babies. A newborn blue whale is about 7 meters long. It drinks milk that's about half fat. And it gains about 90 kilos every single day.
Now, let's bust a myth. You might have heard that a blue whale's heart is the size of a car. And that a child could swim through its arteries. In 2014, a blue whale washed up dead in Canada. For the first time ever, scientists saved its heart and studied it. It weighed about 180 kilos. Big, but smaller than a car. And its biggest artery? Wide enough for a human head, barely. Not for a swimming child.
So if bigger is always better, why don't blue whales get even bigger? In 2019, scientists tagged hundreds of whales and studied more than 10,000 feeding events. For toothed whales, like sperm whales, there's a limit. A bigger sperm whale couldn't find enough big squid to stay alive. For blue whales, bigger still pays. The scientists think what holds them back is the krill season. It's short, and there's only so much food.
And remember that heartbeat from the start? Deep down, it beat just twice a minute. After a dive, it raced up to 37 beats a minute. The scientists think that's close to the most its heart can do. So the heart may be another reason the blue whale is about as big as a whale can get.
So for millions of years, the system works. Then humans show up. In the 20th century, whaling ships with harpoon cannons hunt blue whales across the oceans. Around Antarctica alone, their numbers drop from about 239,000 to around 360. That's almost all of them, gone. In 1960, blue whales are finally protected from commercial hunting. Today, their numbers are growing again. But they're still a small fraction of what they once were.
And there's a new problem. Remember the rule? Blue whales bet everything on krill. And the krill are in trouble. As the Southern Ocean warms and sea ice shrinks, krill around Antarctica have been moving south. Their densest swarms are now about 440 kilometers further south than they were in the 1920s.
And here's a twist. Whales help the krill too. Krill are full of iron. Whales eat krill deep down, then poop near the surface. That puts the iron back into the water. It feeds the tiny plants that krill eat. So when whalers killed the whales, they may have hurt the krill as well.
So why is the blue whale so big? Remember the rule? For a filter feeder, bigger means more food in every mouthful and more fuel for the long trips. Then the Ice Ages packed the oceans with dense swarms of krill. And the blue whale went all in. A wolf-sized hunter on a riverbank in Pakistan became a 150-tonne giant. A giant with a heart that beats twice a minute.
The age of giants didn't end with the dinosaurs. It's happening right now. And whether it continues is now up to us.
